What Do Relation Labels Add to Image–Text Adaptation?

ABSTRACT

An image search system should reject a wrong description and still recognize
different correct descriptions. Training labels can mark a caption as
supported, contradicted, or unresolved by an image. We ask whether using all
three relations helps more than adding supported captions as correct
examples. We compare twelve training rules over one pretrained model's fixed
image and text features, with equal tuning budgets and three runs per setting.
Extra correct examples improve the distinction between supported and
contradicted statements. Gains on edited captions concentrate on added
objects and attributes. Penalizing contradictions more strongly and omitting
unresolved pairs does not improve on the simpler rule. Similarity-based
weights also closely track shuffled or constant weights. The gains come with
lower retrieval accuracy and weaker recognition of valid rewordings. This
comparison tests whether improvements from relation labels carry through
to the task of returning a correct search result.

1. INTRODUCTION

A person searching a photo collection may describe the same scene in several
ways. A photo of a child holding a red ball can match both "a child with a
red ball" and "a red ball held by a child." It should not match
"a child with a blue ball." A useful image–text model must recognize valid
descriptions while distinguishing small changes that make them incorrect.

CLIP learns image and text vectors by scoring associated image–caption pairs
above other candidates [radford2021clip]. Those other captions are not
necessarily wrong. Some describe the image correctly, while others mention
details the image cannot establish. This motivates three relation labels:
supported, contradicted, and neutral. Here, neutral means
that the image leaves the statement unresolved. Such labels suggest a natural
training policy: add supported captions to the positive targets, increase the
penalty on contradictions, and omit neutral pairs.

Each operation also changes the learning problem. Adding positives changes
the target distribution. Weighting negatives changes their relative and total
gradient contributions. Omitting pairs removes competition from the loss.
An improvement over ordinary training therefore need not come from the
specific semantic distinction that motivates a policy. The relevant question
is: what do relation labels add beyond extra positive targets, under
the same data, model, and selection budget?

Prior work gives both the motivation and the necessary comparisons.
Similarity regulation and softened targets address false negatives
[jiang2023regulation, gao2024softclip]; semantic triage and grounded
filtering separate useful from ambiguous pairs
[liu2023sat, byun2024mafa]. On the evaluation side,
Kamath et al. [kamath2024hardpositive] show that gains against difficult wrong
captions can hide sensitivity to valid rewordings, including after generic
positive augmentation. FSC-CLIP studies preservation of broader multimodal
capabilities [oh2024fsc], and CLIC evaluates efficient adaptation on
both discrimination and retrieval [peleg2025clic]. These findings make
retention a necessary outcome of a relation-supervision study.

We reuse one annotated caption pool to compare positive expansion,
contradiction weighting, neutral exclusion, and their controls. All policies
train the same small adapters over frozen CLIP features. This design connects
an intervention-level comparison to three practical tests: rejecting wrong
captions, accepting alternative valid captions, and retrieving a match from
a large candidate pool. The contribution is a controlled account of what
the relation policy adds, supported by complete per-seed results and an
analytical description of how its targets change the update.

2. A CONTROLLED STUDY OF RELATION LABELS

One candidate pool, three interventions.
Every training image has its original source captions and a set of statements
with relation labels from e-SNLI-VE [kayser2021evil]. All contrastive
policies use the same candidate pool. Source positives treats the
five original captions as positive targets and all statement candidates,
including supported ones, as negatives. Positive expansion treats every
label-supported statement as a positive. Starting from that expanded set,
contradiction weighting doubles the weight of labeled contradictions
in the loss, and neutral exclusion removes labeled neutral pairs.
Both combines those two changes. Unannotated cross-image pairs stay
eligible negatives. Neutrality is a relation label, not a model confidence
estimate.

We include count-matched random exclusion, target smoothing, and supervised
pairwise ranking. Four additional policies test text-similarity weighting:
a fixed threshold, its shuffled-weight control, a batch-median threshold,
and a constant negative weight. The shuffle preserves the batch-wide weight
distribution while breaking its association with individual pairs. There
are twelve trainable configurations in total. Appendix A
gives their exact rules and archive names.

Common objective.
Let s_ij be the scaled cosine score of image i and caption j.
For image i, let P_i be its positive-caption set and w_ij a
nonnegative candidate weight. Positive weights are one; zero omits a pair.
The image-to-text loss is

  ell_i = log(sum_j w_ij exp(s_ij)) - (1/|P_i|) sum_{j in P_i} s_ij.   (1)

It gives equal target probability to each positive, following the
mean-log-probability form of multi-positive learning
[khosla2020supervised]. We average it with the corresponding
text-to-image loss over captions having a positive image. The ranking control
uses supported–contradicted pairs instead. Full definitions, including
rectangular batches and the smoothing target, appear in Appendix A.

Training and selection.
We use frozen OpenCLIP ViT-B/32 features from the LAION-2B checkpoint. Two
zero-initialized linear residual maps contain 524,288 trainable parameters;
the encoders and score scale remain fixed. The image-disjoint split contains
1,200 training, 100 calibration, 100 validation, and 400 test images.
Labels follow the released e-SNLI-VE annotation protocol
[do2021esnlive]; provenance is detailed in Appendix B.

Each configuration runs for ten epochs at three learning rates and three
seeds, giving 108 runs. A fixed validation score averages known-positive
image-to-text recall at rank one and supported-versus-contradicted statement
accuracy. Selection includes epoch zero. One learning rate per policy is
chosen across all three seeds, and each seed retains its best validation
epoch. We then evaluate the resulting 36 checkpoints and the frozen
reference. The selection rule and primary contrasts were recorded before
the held-out comparisons.

Four complementary measurements.
On e-SNLI-VE, we measure the fraction of supported statements ranked above
contradicted statements, averaged by image. SugarCrepe tests whether the
original correct caption outranks an edited wrong caption, or foil,
over all 7,511 official pairs [hsieh2023sugarcrepe]. SugarCrepe++ adds a
second valid caption and requires both positives to beat the foil over
4,757 triplets [dumpala2024sugarcrepepp]. Finally, COCO retrieval uses
5,000 images and 25,000 captions. Recall at rank one (R@1) is the percentage
of queries whose highest-scoring candidate is an annotated match.
We evaluate both retrieval directions on the full pool. The two discrimination
measures are primary; retention measures are secondary. Primary intervals use
10,000 paired resamples of images with a Bonferroni adjustment for six planned
contrasts.

The intervals condition on the three selected runs. The combined policy

The transfer gain concentrates in added facts.
SugarCrepe edits either add content, replace it, or swap its assignment.
The aggregate score mixes these different tests. Grouping the saved results

Its improvement comes from rejecting captions with added objects or

The exclusion and weighting controls matter.
Neutral exclusion without contradiction weighting also trails equally
frequent random exclusion by 2.44 points on e-SNLI-VE and 0.86 on SugarCrepe.
This makes a benefit from selecting specifically neutral pairs difficult to
attribute in this setting. Smoothing and pairwise ranking provide additional
simple comparisons in Appendix D.

Discrimination gains have a measurable retrieval cost.
The source-positive adapter already improves SugarCrepe while staying close
to frozen retrieval. Relative to that adapter, positive expansion adds
0.94 points on SugarCrepe but loses 7.53 points in image-to-text R@1.
On the fixed 5,000-image pool, that is about 376 fewer image queries returning
a correct caption first, averaged across the three seeds. Text-to-image
retrieval falls as well. This comparison gives the cost of changing the
positive targets under the tested selection policy.

4. WHAT THE OBJECTIVE CHANGES

Positive labels redistribute a fixed target budget.
Equation 1 distributes one unit of target probability across
the positive set. Suppose K captions are positive and M already-present
candidates become additional positives. Hold the scores, candidate set,
and unit weights fixed. For any original positive p, the loss
derivative changes by

  d ell_expanded/d s_ip - d ell_source/d s_ip = M/[K(K+M)].             (2)

At the logit level, expansion reduces each original positive's target
pressure by this amount. The new positives gain target mass at the
originals' expense. This is a property of the uniform-target
loss, even when every added label is correct. Appendix I
derives the identity, contrasts summed-positive probability, and examines
what negative weights preserve. It supplies an objective-level explanation
for measuring retention alongside discrimination; the learned representation
also depends on shared parameters and checkpoint selection.

Negative weights mix strength and allocation.
The weighting controls address a different ambiguity. For weights held fixed
when differentiating, let the nonempty set N_i contain image i's
nonpositive candidates. At fixed scores and a fixed positive set, define

  c_i* = [sum_{j in N_i} w_ij exp(s_ij)]/[sum_{j in N_i} exp(s_ij)].    (3)

Replacing every negative weight by c_i* leaves the denominator in
Equation 1 unchanged. It therefore preserves the row loss and,
holding c_i* fixed during differentiation, the total gradient on
nonpositive scores. It also preserves every positive score gradient.
Only the allocation of negative gradients changes. A lower weighted loss
can thus reflect weaker overall competition without demonstrating useful
pair-specific semantic assignments. Appendix I gives the proof
and a counterexample showing why a bound on total negative gradient does
not bound each negative group.

This identity defines an analytical reference. Our empirical constant
control instead fixes every negative weight at 0.25. The shuffled control
retains the full batch-wide distribution of similarity weights. These
controls ask whether identifying which pair gets which weight improves
performance beyond nonspecific suppression.

[Table 3: similarity-weighted policies and nonsemantic controls. Values are
in reported_weight_controls.csv. All use source positives.]

Table 3 shows closely tracking scores on both discrimination
tasks and on retrieval. Fixed-threshold weighting and its shuffle select
the same learning rates and epochs in all seeds, as do median-threshold
and constant weighting. The remaining small score differences offer little
evidence of a benefit from pair-specific similarity information here.
Together, the two identities distinguish two changes often grouped under
semantic supervision: assigning target probability and reallocating
competition. Their effects require different controls.

5. IMPLICATIONS FOR IMAGE SEARCH

Relation annotation should be evaluated by the capability it buys. For
the tested adapters, the strongest improvement is on the supervised statement
distinction. The additional gain on edited-caption discrimination is much
smaller, and a large-pool search test exposes a retrieval cost. A practical
evaluation should therefore compare against source-positive adaptation,
include more than one valid wording, and evaluate the candidate pool in which
the model will be used.

Our selection score already includes retrieval, but on the 100-image
e-SNLI-VE validation pool. That term does not ensure retention on a larger
pool from another source. A useful deployment-oriented comparison would
choose checkpoints on a development set representative of the intended
images and queries, then report discrimination and retrieval separately.
This would expose whether a gain fits an application's acceptable retrieval
loss, rather than allowing one metric to compensate silently for the other
in an average. The two objectives can then be compared at a fixed retention
requirement. Retention-aware objectives and richer encoders can be tested
against the same controls. The accompanying configurations, selected states,
and item-level scores support that next comparison.

LIMITATIONS

This study concerns one frozen OpenCLIP checkpoint and linear residual
adaptation on a subset of e-SNLI-VE. It does not establish the behavior of
full-encoder training, other data scales, or other model families. The labels
partly inherit text-inference judgments; the source release does not provide
independent visual verification for every pair. Original caption ownership
also supplies incomplete retrieval relevance.

The tested multi-positive objective averages positive log probabilities.
The summed-positive alternative in Appendix I was not trained.
Independent policy selection changes both the learning rate and retained
epoch; six selections retain the frozen state. The comparisons estimate
selected training procedures. Image-bootstrap intervals condition on three
seeds and do not quantify uncertainty over new training runs. Full
SugarCrepe++, COCO retrieval, and weighting-control differences are secondary
or descriptive outcomes. The added-content versus replacement/swap grouping
is a descriptive summary of saved category scores. SugarCrepe++ shares images
with SugarCrepe, and COCO overlaps other COCO-derived pools. These evaluations
measure different capabilities rather than independent replications. Detailed
overlaps, annotation provenance, source defects, and analysis choices are
recorded in the appendix. No evaluation measures live search users or a new
deployment domain.

ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS AND ASSISTANCE

We use existing public datasets under their source terms. The study does not
collect new human judgments. Image captions and pretrained features may
reflect source biases. Generative AI assisted research planning, code,
analysis, literature discovery, and manuscript drafting. Claims are tied to
the archived computations and cited sources; assistance does not replace
author responsibility for verification.
